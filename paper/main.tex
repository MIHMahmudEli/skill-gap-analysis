% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Computing curricula and employer demands are said to diverge, but neither has been measured at scale. We compare a 1.35-million-posting general-occupation LinkedIn corpus (one scrape family, not computing-only) with an employer-derived occupational reference profile (1,167 postings, five categories); analyses use its 518,953 skill-annotated postings, re-checked on a 1.29-million superset. N-gram extraction over an eight-category taxonomy combines Sentence-BERT alignment (τ ∈ {0.4–0.7}; 1,000-fold bootstrap). The Skill Gap Index (SGI) is the share of top-100 posting skills with no match in the reference profile. Over registered sweeps it spans 0.29 to 0.73 lexically and 0.04 to 0.42 at semantic $\tau = 0.5$, falling from 0.32 to 0.04 as the covering set grows from 100 to all 5,047 skills; only matched $(K, P)$ comparisons are interpretable. In the headline setting (market top-100 vs.\ occupational top-100) exact matching yields 52.0\%; semantic alignment cuts this by 20 points to 32.0\% at $\tau = 0.5$ (95\% CI [23.0\%, 41.0\%]). The profile covers 30.4\% of CS2023—a computational anchor, not a human audit—so no curricular gap is claimed. Deficits concentrate in Cloud Infrastructure (2.45/0.94 pp) and Programming \& Development (1.69 pp lexical); soft skills are fully covered. Agreement with expert ESCO mappings is $\kappa = 0.737$ (91.9\% precision, 75.7\% recall; software-skewed gold set), consistent with vocabulary divergence. A taxonomy-derived O\*NET$\times$BLS profile shares just 1.0\% of its 8,613 terms with the reference profile: vocabulary- and scope-dominated, not comparable at skill or category level. SGI levels depend on these design choices and must be reported with their settings.
\end{abstract}

Skill-gap measurement, Job-posting analytics, Employability, Natural Language Processing, Sentence-BERT, Measurement validity, Data provenance.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{echnological} developments across distributed cloud architectures, data engineering, and artificial intelligence have altered employer skill requirements across major economic sectors. Because university curricula are updated through multi-year institutional review cycles, curricula and market skill demands plausibly change at different rates---a difference this paper measures rather than presumes \cite{jaiswal2024understanding, jumaan2026understanding}. Educational researchers and labor economists label this \textit{perceived} difference the \textit{skills gap}---defined as the divergence between competencies taught in degree programs and the practical proficiencies sought by employers \cite{febriani2025employability, barachino2025operationalization}. We treat that divergence as a quantity to be measured, not as an established fact.

Reports from the World Economic Forum \cite{wef2020} indicate that over 40\% of core worker skills are expected to shift within five-year windows, particularly in artificial intelligence, cloud architectures, and data engineering. In addition to specialized technical skills, employer surveys frequently report \textit{perceived} shortfalls in transferable competencies such as professional communication and collaborative problem-solving \cite{tight2023employability, braun2026concept, noor2024skills}. 

Prior empirical work on curriculum-to-market comparison exhibits four main limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{Limited Sample Scope:} Most studies rely on institutional surveys, small employer samples ($N < 500$), or single-nation case studies \cite{baral2026analyzing, alhazmi2025aligning}, offering limited visibility into cross-national patterns.
    \item \textbf{Methodological Separation:} Investigations typically employ qualitative coding \cite{rikala2024understanding} or predictive classification without semantic text analysis, leaving unresolved whether reported gaps reflect distinct vocabularies or absent skills \cite{kruger2025improving, lai2025learning}.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Relevance:} Several widely cited baseline studies pre-date recent enterprise developments, including widespread cloud migration and the deployment of generative AI tools (2022--2026) \cite{anthuvan2026reverse, sun2025creative}.
    \item \textbf{Limited Theoretical Grounding:} Computational employability models are frequently reported without reference to economic or educational frameworks that would explain \textit{why} curricula and market demands might diverge---frameworks used here to interpret measurements, not to presume their outcome \cite{schlippe2023skill, eimer2023employability}.
\end{enumerate}

We address three research questions, each answered against the seven sources that feed the experiments reported here (LinkedIn, HuggingFace, and public benchmark repositories); the wider 15-dataset inventory those sources sit inside, and the reason the gap findings rest on a 1.35M-posting subset of it, is described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} and Table~\ref{tab:datasets}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Market Topography):} What are the most frequent skill requirements in online job postings?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Lexical versus Semantic Alignment):} To what degree do occupational reference-profile descriptors align with market requirements when evaluated using exact string matching versus dense semantic vector embeddings?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Domain-Level Reference-Profile Deficits):} What is the magnitude of the reference-profile coverage deficit across distinct subject areas (Programming, Data Science, Cloud Infrastructure, Soft Skills, Cybersecurity)?
\end{itemize}

Placement-benchmark analyses (employability determinants and cross-cohort placement dynamics; experiments STAT01, STAT02, PLAC01, INTL01) are reported in the companion benchmark-audit article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}); the geographic distribution of postings (GEO01) is reported within RQ1 above.

\textbf{Contributions:} This work makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Provenance-Audited Corpus for the Instrument:} We assemble the posting corpus on which every measurement in this article runs---the 1.35M-posting LinkedIn scrape and its 519{,}814-posting nested skills subset, plus the separate 1{,}167-posting occupational reference profile---and audit all 15 inventoried sources for duplication and generator signatures (\texttt{datasets/OVERLAP\_AUDIT.csv}, \texttt{datasets/REAL\_VS\_SYNTHETIC.csv}). That audit is what excludes the 316K aggregated title--skill rows and the 458K benchmark rows of unverified provenance, including 421K student/alumni records and one file its own source declares synthetic, from every gap measurement reported here (Appendix~\ref{app:sources}); the instrument is thereby run only on web-scraped posting rows.
    \item \textbf{Dual Lexical--Semantic Analysis:} Using Sentence-BERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) alongside exact string matching, we show that exact matching yields a lexical SGI of 52.0\%---a measurement, not a curricular gap size---whereas semantic vector alignment lowers the estimate to 32.0\% at $\tau = 0.5$.
    \item \textbf{Externally Validated Measurement Instrument:} Beyond in-sample estimates, we validate the matcher against published expert ESCO gold mappings ($\kappa = 0.737$) and re-check both gap indices on a nested-superset robustness sample of 1.29M postings from the same scrape family (a sample-size extension, not an independent replication); a separate computational anchor (embedding coverage, not human audit) relates the occupational reference profile to the CS2023 curricular Body of Knowledge.
    \item \textbf{Threshold Sensitivity Estimation:} We compute 1,000-fold bootstrap confidence intervals across four similarity thresholds ($\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$) to evaluate how threshold selection affects gap measurement.
    \item \textbf{Theory-Informed Interpretation:} Human Capital Theory and Constructive Alignment (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) are used only to derive the three pre-specified checks of Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}, each registered against at least one experiment and reported with its verdict in Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses}; both are interpretive lenses rather than premises of the instrument, so the measurement claims stand independently of any theory. Because this study measures a posting-derived reference profile rather than curricula, it derives no recommendation about course design or assessment; provenance and limitations are documented alongside the measurements.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this article is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work and outlines the theoretical framework. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the datasets, text processing pipeline, and statistical methods. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical results for RQ1--RQ3. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses theoretical implications, an institutional reading of the measurements, reproducibility, and threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the study. The placement-model experiments (STAT01, STAT02, PLAC01, INTL01) are audited in a companion article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}).

\section{Related Work and Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Conceptualizations of the Skills Gap (Literature)}

The term \textit{skills gap} is used across macroeconomic, institutional and pedagogical traditions with no settled operational definition---an ambiguity that motivates measuring it explicitly. Conceptualizations differ substantially across stakeholders: industry surveys often define gaps in terms of immediate job-readiness, whereas university leaders emphasize broad intellectual development and foundational theory \cite{braun2026concept, tight2023employability}. Digital transformation has also increased demand for cross-disciplinary competencies, such as data literacy among non-computing professionals \cite{rikala2024understanding}.

Computational curriculum analytics has sought to quantify these dynamics using course descriptions and accreditation criteria. Cuckov et al. \cite{cuckov2022quantifying} evaluated computer engineering courses against ABET criteria, finding delays in updating upper-level electives. Zhao et al. \cite{zhao2026proximity} measured structural proximity between academic research and patent descriptions using knowledge graphs. Focusing on software engineering, Korrapati et al. \cite{korrapati2025aligning} and Alhazmi et al. \cite{alhazmi2025aligning} examined curriculum alignment with cloud computing and AI requirements, noting that elective coursework often trails industry tool adoption.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}

Two theoretical perspectives inform the interpretation of these empirical observations:

\textbf{1) Human Capital Theory (HCT):} Formulated by Becker \cite{becker1964human} and Schultz \cite{schultz1961investment}, HCT posits that education enhances productive capacity, yielding labor-market returns. HCT distinguishes between \textit{general human capital} (transferable competencies such as critical thinking, communication, and core mathematical reasoning) and \textit{firm-specific human capital} (proficiency with specialized software tools, proprietary APIs, or operational frameworks). Universities are commonly characterised as prioritizing general human capital because their missions center on enduring principles; we use this as an interpretive lens, not as a premise of the measurement. Conversely, employer job descriptions frequently specify immediately applicable technical tools. This distinction helps explain why measured reference-profile deficits concentrate in applied toolchains such as cloud platforms and data libraries.

\textbf{2) Constructive Alignment \& Curriculum Theory:} Biggs \cite{biggs1996enhancing} introduced constructive alignment to establish coherence among Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs), teaching methods, and assessment tasks. At an institutional level, any measured divergence can be read through constructive alignment as \textit{external misalignment}---ILOs are governed by multi-year accreditation reviews (such as ABET and ACM/IEEE guidelines), whereas industry skill demands evolve on shorter market cycles. We report this as one interpretation of the measurements, not as a premise of the instrument.

\subsection{Natural Language Processing for Skill-Gap Measurement}

Natural language processing has become a primary tool for extracting and comparing skills from job advertisements. Skill identification from postings is itself an established research program, survey-driven since Khaouja et al. \cite{khaouja2021survey}, and evaluated against human-annotated benchmark corpora whose annotation schemes distinguish skills from qualifications, experience, and occupation \cite{green2022development, zhang2022skillspan}. Recent deep-learning surveys catalogue the datasets and the persistent inconsistency in hard/soft-skill terminology that motivates semantic over lexical comparison \cite{senger2024deep}. While keyword extraction is widely used, rule-based matching often fails on nuanced occupational phrases \cite{kruger2025improving}. Pre-trained language representations address this limitation by projecting terms into continuous vector spaces. For instance, Baral and Ghimire \cite{baral2026analyzing} used embeddings to measure syllabus alignment in Nepal, showing that semantic representations bridge terminology differences between academic syllabi and job listings. Text-mining methods have similarly been applied to profile data science roles \cite{mol2024unravelling, tee2024demand} and build resume-matching systems \cite{daberao2025resumeinsight}. In this work, we use Sentence-BERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) \cite{reimers2019sentence} to quantify alignment between market requirements and reference-profile descriptors across multiple similarity thresholds, and we validate the resulting matcher against expert mappings rather than assuming its accuracy.

\subsection{Pre-Specified Checks (H1--H3)}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The two theoretical perspectives of Section~\ref{sec:theory} yield three pre-specified checks, each registered against at least one experiment before that experiment's results existed (Table~\ref{tab:experiments}). We call them \emph{checks} rather than hypotheses because they do not carry equal evidential force, and because two of the three are close to guaranteed by design; saying so here keeps the verdict table in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} from reading as three independent tests of equal severity. First, \textbf{H1 tests the reference profile, not curricula}: the instrument reads a posting-derived profile and no curriculum is observed, so H1 asks whether that profile leaves a deficit in technical categories relative to market demand. The curricular reading of a deficit---that a curriculum lags---is an interpretation layered on top of the measurement, which is why H1 is reported as \emph{consistent with} the measurements rather than as confirmed. Second, \textbf{H3 is near-guaranteed by construction}: the ALIGN04 superset is a strict superset produced by the same scrape pipeline, so it re-observes most of the primary corpus rather than sampling independently of it, and a strictly positive SGI would additionally require the two top-$K$ vocabularies to coincide almost exactly. H3 is therefore a check that the instrument does not break under sample-size extension within a single scrape family, not a prediction that plausibly could have failed. H2 carries the substantive directional content.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*:}]
    \item \textbf{Constructive Alignment (external misalignment):} Because Intended Learning Outcomes are governed by accreditation cycles while toolchains evolve continuously, curricular coverage should be complete for enduring general competencies (soft skills) but incomplete for applied technical toolchains (Cloud, Programming, Data Science) \cite{biggs1996enhancing}. \textit{Test:} domain deficits in ALIGN03 and their nested-superset re-check in ALIGN04.
    \item \textbf{Terminology divergence (measurement):} If a share of the reported gap is vocabulary mismatch rather than absent competence \cite{kruger2025improving}, dense semantic matching should remove a substantial share of the lexically measured gap, and the matches it adds beyond exact string matching should be predominantly valid. Because every exact string match is also a cosine match at $\tau \le 0.7$, the \emph{direction} of the change is guaranteed by construction; the hypotheses concern the \emph{magnitude} of the reduction (with bootstrap confidence intervals) and the validity of the added matches. \textit{Tests:} ALIGN01 vs.\ ALIGN02 (magnitude of reduction); ALIGN04 (nested superset) and CURR01 (a third vocabulary) for the same question; VALID01 (expert gold) for the validity of the additional matches.
    \item \textbf{Instrument robustness:} If the Skill Gap Index measures a property of the profile--market pair rather than of one sample of it, both variants of SGI should remain strictly positive and the magnitude of the semantic reduction should persist when the market sample is extended from the 519K-posting primary corpus to a nested superset of the same posting-scrape family. \textit{Test:} ALIGN04 (LinkedIn nested superset, $N = 1.29$M postings).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Data Corpus and Provenance Architecture}

The corpus comprises 15 open datasets acquired in September 2026 from Kaggle and HuggingFace repositories under open-access licenses (CC0, CC-BY 4.0, and ODbL). Sources carry per-source observation and release windows listed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}; the regional profiles are calibrated against the India Skills Report 2026 (that source declares itself synthetic). Summing raw file row counts yields 3,163,517 records; a provenance audit (\texttt{datasets/OVERLAP\_AUDIT.csv}) then shows that the two HuggingFace Skill Gap tables share an identical set of 519,814 \texttt{job\_link} identifiers (a linked pair, not two samples) and that this entire pair is a strict subset of the LinkedIn 2024 postings table (all 519,814 links present), de-duplicating to 2,123,889 unique record rows. That total pools four incompatible row units and is therefore not used as an all-purpose evidence count anywhere in this paper: 1,349,621 real web-scraped posting rows (the 1,348,454 LinkedIn postings plus a separate 1,167-posting occupational-reference file; the 519,814 nested HuggingFace postings contribute no additional rows), 519,814 posting--skill pair rows that vanish on de-duplication, 316,279 aggregated title--skill count rows of unverifiable provenance, and 457,989 benchmark rows of unverified provenance (445,989 signature-flagged---a 10,345-row AI job series with strong generator signatures, a 15,000-row AI salary file with moderate signatures, and 420,644 student/alumni records with moderate-to-weak signatures---plus 12,000 India-skill profiles declared synthetic by their source; evidence tiers are computed from the share of applicable discriminating checks fired, in \texttt{datasets/REAL\_VS\_SYNTHETIC.csv} and \texttt{datasets/synthetic\_checks\_output.json}). Table~\ref{tab:datasets} reports the same accounting grouped by row unit. The occupational-reference file contributes 1,167 job postings rather than a separate 13,833-row taxonomy. Of the 15 sources, eight feed an experiment and seven feed none. Four of the eight feed this article---the LinkedIn root and the two HuggingFace Skill Gap tables, plus the separate 1,167-posting reference file (the row-unit accounting above treats Jobs and Skills as one linked pair)---and four feed only the placement-audit experiments of the companion article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}). The seven that feed none are the two AI series, the India Skills file that its own source declares synthetic, the 316K title$\times$skill aggregate, and three unused student/alumni benchmarks; they are retained for inventory completeness and listed in Appendix~\ref{app:sources}, alongside the generator-signature tier in \texttt{datasets/REAL\_VS\_SYNTHETIC.csv} and the duplication statistics in \texttt{datasets/OVERLAP\_AUDIT.csv} (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the four sources behind every result reported below, with sample sizes, observation windows, and licenses; Appendix~\ref{app:sources} does the same for the remaining eleven.

To avoid pooling datasets with incompatible collection mechanisms, we divide the corpus into two functional tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Online Labor Market Postings (1.35M real web-scraped postings; 316K unverified aggregate rows; the linked HuggingFace pair contributes no unique rows):} Comprises the LinkedIn dataset (1.35M records) \cite{asaniczka2024linkedin}, the USA Job Market dataset (316K records) \cite{batuhanklac2024usa}, and the linked HuggingFace Skill Gap jobs--skills pair (519K postings with skill annotations), which the provenance audit shows to be a strict subset of the LinkedIn postings table and which therefore contributes no additional unique records. These corpora contain job advertisements from North America, Europe, and the Asia-Pacific region, providing direct observations of employer skill specifications.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: Student Placement and Career Benchmarks ($N \approx 421\text{K}$ student/alumni benchmark rows of unverified provenance, moderate-to-none generator signatures; no verified real graduate records):} Comprises College Student Placement ($N = 10,000$) \cite{sumedh15072024college}, NGESS Graduate Employment ($N = 7,200$) \cite{philipdayo2024ngess}, Global Student Placement ($N = 10,000$) \cite{rakesh6302024global}, Student Career Success ($N = 50,000$) \cite{mobeenfatimah2024career}, International Graduates ($N = 300,000$) \cite{quackquackrp2024international}, Field-of-Study vs.\ Occupation ($N = 38,444$) \cite{jahnavipaliwal2024field}, and India Fresher Market ($N = 5,000$) \cite{shambhurajejagadale2024india} ($420{,}644$ records in total). These tabular records provide academic, behavioral, and placement indicators; the placement-benchmark experiments built on them are audited in the companion article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Supplementary Demand-Side Source: O\*NET~30.0 and BLS OEWS (ALIGN06)}
\label{sec:align06_data}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} inventories the posting corpora behind every headline result. A supplementary demand-side check, experiment ALIGN06, draws instead on two US government sources containing no postings at all. The first is the \textbf{O\*NET~30.0 Database} (release \texttt{db\_30\_0}, August 2025): its \textit{Technology Skills} table (2.B.1.a; 32,681 occupation$\times$technology rows over 1,016 O\*NET-SOC occupations), plus its \textit{Skills} table (2.A.3.a, Scale ID \texttt{IM}) for one weighting variant. O\*NET~30.0 Database is used under the \textbf{CC BY 4.0} licence: ``This analysis uses information from the O\*NET~30.0 Database by the U.S. Department of Labor, Employment and Training Administration (USDOL/ETA). Used under the CC BY 4.0 license. O\*NET\textsuperscript{\textregistered} is a trademark of USDOL/ETA.'' The second is the \textbf{BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics}, May 2025 national estimates (workbook \texttt{national\_M2025\_dl.xlsx}), supplying employment weights $w(o)$ for 830 detailed occupations: ``Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS), May 2025 national estimates.'' OEWS is a US federal work in the public domain (17 U.S.C.~\S105); the BLS emblem is not used. Both archives are pinned by SHA-256 in ALIGN06 \texttt{metrics.json}; licence snapshots are archived under \texttt{datasets/curriculum/license\_snapshots/}.

The profile truncates each O\*NET-SOC code to its SOC six-digit base, joins that base to the corresponding OEWS occupation, and weights each term by the employment of every occupation listing it (Section~\ref{sec:align06}). It is a \emph{taxonomy-derived} inventory of tools and technologies, not a sample of postings; the market side of every headline result remains the posting corpora of Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

\subsection{Taxonomy Construction and Skill Extraction}

We define an eight-category occupational skill taxonomy $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_8\}$ synthesized from the ACM/IEEE CS2023 curriculum guidelines and O*NET descriptors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Programming \& Development:} Python, Java, C++, JavaScript, SQL, TypeScript, Go, Rust, React, Docker, Kubernetes, Git.
    \item \textbf{Data Science \& Analytics:} Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Statistical Analysis, PyTorch, TensorFlow, NLP, Computer Vision.
    \item \textbf{Cloud \& Infrastructure:} AWS, Azure, GCP, CI/CD Pipelines, Terraform, Linux Administration, Serverless Architectures.
    \item \textbf{Soft Skills:} Professional Communication, Leadership, Teamwork, Critical Thinking, Time Management, Adaptability.
    \item \textbf{Business \& Domain:} Agile/Scrum, Product Management, Requirements Gathering, Business Analysis, Risk Compliance.
    \item \textbf{Design \& UX:} UI/UX Design, Figma, User Research, Wireframing, Prototyping, Usability Testing.
    \item \textbf{Data Engineering:} ETL Pipelines, Apache Airflow, Apache Kafka, Distributed Data Warehousing, PostgreSQL, NoSQL.
    \item \textbf{Cybersecurity:} Network Security, Penetration Testing, Incident Response, SIEM Systems, ISO 27001, Cryptography.
\end{enumerate}

Skill extraction from text is conducted using regularized n-gram tokenization matched against taxonomy dictionaries, as summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:skill_extract}. Occupational skill requirements are represented using a standardized occupational--educational reference profile (job-facing skill lists from five occupational categories) rather than scraped syllabi---a proxy whose content validity is quantified against CS2023 in CURR01 (Section~\ref{sec:curr01}); profile-based matching without scraped syllabi follows established practice in semantic resume--job systems \cite{daberao2025resumeinsight}. Extraction and matching are fully automated, with matcher validity established externally against expert ESCO annotations rather than in-house re-annotation (Section~\ref{sec:valid01}).

\textbf{Reference-profile circularity check.} Because the reference profile and the market corpus are both posting-derived, we checked whether the same postings occur on both sides rather than assuming independence. \textbf{Primary, title-free.} An exact set-similarity join over per-posting skill sets compares all $1{,}167$ profile postings against all $515{,}996$ distinct market skill sets ($518{,}953$ of the $519{,}814$ primary-corpus postings carry skills) with no title restriction, and finds \emph{no profile/market pairs at skill-set Jaccard $\geq 0.7$ regardless of title} (maximum $0.667$; $0$ pairs at $\geq 0.9$). \textbf{Secondary, title-join.} A normalized-title join followed by a title--skill-set Jaccard comparison finds $0$ of the $1{,}167$ profile postings at title--skill Jaccard $\geq 0.9$; the single collision at Jaccard $\geq 0.5$ involves only the generic title ``retail sales manager''. The looser title-only join matches $575$ of $1{,}167$, but these are shared job titles---occupations that exist in both corpora---rather than shared postings, and their skill sets diverge: of the $575$, none reaches Jaccard $\geq 0.9$ and $574$ fall below $0.5$ ($410$ between $0.1$ and $0.5$, $164$ below $0.1$, and $1$ between $0.5$ and $0.9$). Because a title join can only compare postings whose titles already agree, the title-free join above---which reads no title field at all---is the check that would catch a duplicate whose title differed; the title-join figures are reported as the secondary, more permissive diagnostic. Full counts and the pre-specified leave-out sensitivity design are in \texttt{reports/CIRCULARITY\_CHECK.md}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Semantic Skill Extraction and Embedding Alignment}
\label{alg:skill_extract}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Market text corpus $\mathcal{D}_M$, Occupational reference-profile corpus $\mathcal{D}_A$, Taxonomy $\mathcal{C}$, Sentence-BERT model $\mathcal{E}$, Threshold $\tau$
\ENSURE Market skill set $\mathcal{M}$, Occupational skill set $\mathcal{A}$, Similarity matrix $\mathbf{S}$, Gap Index $\text{SGI}(\tau)$
\STATE $\mathcal{M} \leftarrow \emptyset, \mathcal{A} \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FORALL{$d \in \mathcal{D}_M$}
    \STATE Extract normalized skill tokens $T_d \leftarrow \text{Tokenize}(d, \mathcal{C})$
    \STATE $\mathcal{M} \leftarrow \mathcal{M} \cup T_d$
\ENDFOR
\FORALL{$d \in \mathcal{D}_A$}
    \STATE Extract normalized reference-profile tokens $T_a \leftarrow \text{Tokenize}(d, \mathcal{C})$
    \STATE $\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \mathcal{A} \cup T_a$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Filter $\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}} \leftarrow \text{Top-}K(\mathcal{M}), \mathcal{A}_{\text{top}} \leftarrow \text{Top-}P(\mathcal{A})$ \COMMENT{$K{=}100$; $P{\in}\{100,500\}$ by experiment (Sec.~III)} 
\STATE Compute dense embeddings: $\mathbf{E}_M \leftarrow \mathcal{E}(\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}), \mathbf{E}_A \leftarrow \mathcal{E}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}})$
\STATE Compute pairwise cosine similarity:
\STATE $\mathbf{S}_{ij} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{e}_{m,i} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{a,j}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{m,i}\|_2 \|\mathbf{e}_{a,j}\|_2} \quad \forall m_i \in \mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}, a_j \in \mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}$
\FORALL{$i \in \{1, \dots, |\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}|\}$}
    \STATE $\sigma_i \leftarrow \max_j \mathbf{S}_{ij}$
    \STATE $\text{covered}_i \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(\sigma_i \ge \tau)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\text{SGI}(\tau) \leftarrow 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}|} \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}|} \text{covered}_i$
\RETURN $\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}, \mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}, \mathbf{S}, \text{SGI}(\tau)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Semantic Vector Alignment via Sentence-BERT}

Exact string matching can overestimate profile--market gaps when the occupational reference profile and job postings use different wording for equivalent skills (for example, ``interpersonal communication'' versus ``client communication skills''). To account for semantic similarity, we use the Sentence-BERT model (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) \cite{reimers2019sentence}.

Let $\mathbf{e}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ denote the normalized dense vector embedding of market skill $s \in \mathcal{M}$, and let $\mathbf{e}_{s'} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ denote the embedding of occupational reference-profile descriptor $s' \in \mathcal{A}$. The semantic alignment score is given by:
\begin{equation}
\text{sim}(s, s') = \frac{\mathbf{e}_s \cdot \mathbf{e}_{s'}}{\|\mathbf{e}_s\|_2 \|\mathbf{e}_{s'}\|_2}
\label{eq:cosine}
\end{equation}

A market skill $s$ is designated as covered at threshold $\tau \in [0, 1]$ if:
\begin{equation}
\text{Covered}(s; \tau) = \mathbb{I}\left(\max_{s' \in \mathcal{A}} \text{sim}(s, s') \ge \tau\right)
\label{eq:covered}
\end{equation}

The Skill Gap Index (SGI) at threshold $\tau$ is defined as the complement of the mean coverage rate across the market skill space:
\begin{equation}
\text{SGI}(\tau) = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{M}} \text{Covered}(s; \tau)
\label{eq:sgi_tau}
\end{equation}

To estimate empirical uncertainty without parametric distribution assumptions, we implement a 1,000-fold non-parametric bootstrap over the binary coverage indicator array with fixed random seed ($\text{seed} = 42$), computing 95\% percentile confidence intervals:
\begin{equation}
\text{CI}_{95\%} = \left[\hat{\theta}^*_{(0.025)}, \hat{\theta}^*_{(0.975)}\right]
\label{eq:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
We use $\tau = 0.5$ as the baseline threshold and evaluate sensitivity at $\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$. The market profile size is fixed at $K = 100$ throughout. The reference-profile covering-set size is $P = 100$ for the headline SGI experiments (ALIGN01/02) and $P = 500$ for the domain-deficit and nested-superset experiments (ALIGN03/04) and CURR01; Section~\ref{sec:align04} reports a matched-profile sensitivity that shows how SGI level varies with $P$. We use $P$ for this covering-set size alone and write $n_{\text{post}}$ for a market posting sample size, the two being swept by different experiments (Sec.~\ref{sec:statinf}).

\subsection{Statistical Inference}
\label{sec:statinf}

All interval estimates reported in this article are 95\% percentile intervals from the 1{,}000-fold non-parametric bootstrap defined in Equation~\ref{eq:bootstrap} (seed 42).

\textbf{What the headline interval resamples.} The bootstrap of Equation~\ref{eq:bootstrap} draws with replacement from the $|\mathcal{M}| = K = 100$ binary coverage indicators---that is, from the \emph{market skill list}, not from postings. The headline intervals ($[0.42, 0.63]$ lexical; $[0.23, 0.41]$ semantic at $\tau = 0.5$) therefore answer the question ``how far would the answer move if a different top-$K$ skill list had topped the frequency ranking?'', and are silent on ``how far would it move if a different sample of postings had been observed?''. Because these are distinct uncertainties, the pre-registered dual bootstrap of SENS01 estimates the second one separately: it resamples postings and, with them, the 1{,}167 reference-profile rows, and rebuilds both top-$K$ profiles inside every replicate. At its largest posting draw ($n_{\text{post}} = 1{,}000$, $K = 100$) it reproduces the headline point estimates exactly ($0.52$ lexical; $0.32$ semantic at $\tau = 0.5$) and returns posting-level 95\% intervals of $[0.49, 0.58]$ and $[0.28, 0.38]$ respectively, against skill-list intervals of $[0.43, 0.62]$ and $[0.23, 0.42]$ computed on the same cells (the 0.01 offset from the headline values is the expected method- rather than bit-replication of Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Both posting-level intervals sit \emph{inside} their skill-list counterparts, so resampling the skill list is the conservative direction here: at the same posting draw, asking which skills ranked is the larger source of uncertainty. SENS01 deliberately reports no posting-level interval at the full-corpus ($n_{\text{post}} = \text{all}$) anchor rows, where the headline numbers come from; resampling all 518{,}953 postings per replicate\footnote{The HF Skill Gap skills table lists 519{,}814 postings; 861 of them record no skill at all and are dropped by the loader, so the analysis and every market-side resample operate on 518{,}953, the count ALIGN03 reports as \texttt{n\_postings}. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} records both numbers.} is beyond SENS01's gather implementation, and the posting component of the interval can only shrink as the sample grows, so the full-corpus posting-level term is bounded by $[0.28, 0.38]$ rather than exceeding it. SENS02 computes that term directly with a sparse form of the same resample and finds it inside the bound ($[0.50, 0.55]$ lexical; $[0.30, 0.34]$ semantic at $\tau = 0.5$). Group-comparison statistics for the placement cohort (Welch's $t$-test, Cohen's $d$, Cram\'er's $V$, and variance-inflation diagnostics) are defined, executed, and reported in the companion benchmark-audit article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}), together with the classifier and decision-boundary diagnostics; they play no role in the alignment results reported here.

\subsubsection{External Validation Against Expert Gold Standards (VALID01)}
\label{sec:valid01}

Because all alignment metrics rest on the skill matcher, the matcher itself
is validated against an independent, human-annotated gold standard rather
than in-house labels: the skill-extraction benchmark of
\cite{decorte2022negative}, whose skill mentions in StackOverflow and
public-sector job postings were manually mapped to ESCO skills
\cite{zhang2022skillspan, esco2026} by domain experts (CC-BY-4.0). The
protocol (experiment VALID01; seed 42) forms 1,062 expert-mapped mentions
from the TECH and HOUSE test splits into 1,138 positive (mention, skill)
pairs and 1,769 negatives---one random unmapped ESCO skill per mention plus
sibling-mention skills as in-sentence hard negatives---for 2,907 pairs in
total. Lexical rules agree with the expert mappings only slightly (exact
match $\kappa = 0.112$, 95\% CI [0.091, 0.132]; containment $\kappa =
0.263$), whereas the Sentence-BERT matcher reaches $\kappa = 0.737$ [0.711,
0.760] at the baseline $\tau = 0.5$ (accuracy $87.9\%$ [86.6\%, 89.0\%],
precision $91.9\%$, recall $75.7\%$, ROC-AUC of the cosine score $0.961$;
Table~\ref{tab:validity}). Agreement remains substantial across the
operating range ($\kappa$ from $0.79$ at $\tau = 0.4$ to $0.44$ at $\tau =
0.7$), and the near-chance lexical $\kappa$ is consistent with this section's
central reading---that exact string matching resolves substantially less than
the expert mappings do, which is why the semantic SGI is the headline
instrument---but it does not corroborate that reading \emph{independently}:
the pair-level $\kappa$ and the corpus-level SGI contrast are two views of the
same matcher, so VALID01 validates the component that produces the headline
rather than confirming the headline from outside.

\textbf{Scope of the precision figure.} The pair set is 39.1\% positive by
construction (1,138 positive pairs of 2,907), and its negatives are a mix of
one random unmapped ESCO skill per mention and sibling-mention skills drawn
from the same sentence. Precision is prevalence-dependent and falls as
positives become rarer, so the reported $91.9\%$ is a property of this
near-balanced test set and is an \emph{upper} bound on deployment, where the
matcher scores $K \times P$ pairs---$10{,}000$ of them at the headline $K = P
= 100$---and true correspondences are a small minority of them. The figure
should therefore be read as evidence that the matcher ranks expert-mapped
pairs well under a 39.1\% base rate, not as the share of matches the
instrument reports that would be correct in the 100 $\times$ $P$ setting.

A complementary vocabulary check against the 13,896-skill ESCO
inventory shows that only 6\% of the market Top-100 and 11\% of the
occupational Top-100 appear verbatim in ESCO, yet dense matching at $\tau =
0.5$ resolves 91\% [85\%, 96\%] and 99\% [97\%, 100\%] of them to an ESCO
skill under paraphrase---evidence that the extracted vocabularies are
semantically grounded in an expert-curated taxonomy.

\subsubsection{Nested-Superset Robustness Check on LinkedIn (ALIGN04)}
\label{sec:align04}

A single-sample instrument invites the objection that the measured gap is an artefact of one draw from the market. A pairwise provenance audit shows that the primary 519,814-posting corpus is a strict subset of the LinkedIn 2024 scrape (every \texttt{job\_link} present), so Experiment ALIGN04 functions as a nested-superset robustness check---a sample-size extension within one scrape family, not an independent replication---by re-running the lexical and semantic procedures on the larger LinkedIn sample ($N = 1{,}294{,}296$ postings with skill annotations), with the occupational covering set at $P = 500$ to match ALIGN03 (top-100 market profile, seed 42, 1,000-fold bootstrap). H2 and H3 receive direct tests under this extension. Exact matching yields SGI $= 0.37$ [0.28, 0.47] and dense matching at $\tau = 0.5$ yields SGI $= 0.14$ [0.07, 0.21]; both confidence intervals exclude zero, and the lexical-above-semantic ordering and the monotone increase with $\tau$ match the primary-corpus pattern (Table~\ref{tab:crosscorpus}). Sweeping the full $(K, n_{\text{post}})$ grid ($K \in \{50, 100, 200, 500\}$, $n_{\text{post}} \in \{100, 250, 500, 1000\}$) on this nested superset keeps lexical SGI within $[0.53, 0.74]$ and semantic SGI at $\tau = 0.5$ within $[0.26, 0.38]$, against $[0.52, 0.73]$ and $[0.26, 0.42]$ on the primary corpus---so both ranges are corpus-scoped properties of the grid rather than universal constants.

\textbf{Profile-size sensitivity (SENS02).} Because ALIGN01/02 use reference-profile $P = 100$ while ALIGN03/04 use $P = 500$, the raw level contrast (HF $0.52/0.32$ vs.\ LinkedIn $0.37/0.14$) confounds corpus with covering-set size. The matched-profile re-estimate used only those two sizes, so we pre-registered SENS02 to sweep the covering-set size alone: $K = 100$ and the full market corpus held fixed, $P \in \{100, 250, 500, 1000, \text{all}\}$ (``all'' $=$ all $5{,}047$ skills of the $1{,}167$-row reference profile), on both corpora, lexically and at $\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$, with a skill-list bootstrap and a posting-level bootstrap that rebuilds both profiles inside each of $500$ replicates. Lexical SGI falls monotonically $0.52 \rightarrow 0.43 \rightarrow 0.37 \rightarrow 0.34 \rightarrow 0.29$ and semantic SGI at $\tau = 0.5$ falls $0.32 \rightarrow 0.20 \rightarrow 0.14 \rightarrow 0.11 \rightarrow 0.04$; the two corpora agree at every size (cross-corpus gap $0.00$; the $0.52 \rightarrow 0.37$ change is $0.15$ on both corpora). That agreement is arithmetic, not a finding about the market: at every swept $K \le 200$ the two corpora' top-$K$ market skill lists are literally identical (shared fraction and Jaccard both $1.00$; \texttt{reports/sens01\_market\_profile\_overlap.csv}), so any instrument scoring the same market list against the same covering set must return the same number. The identity therefore carries no evidence that the two markets are alike---and it is correspondingly weak support for H3---but it does locate the $0.52 \rightarrow 0.37$ shift on the covering-set axis rather than on the corpus axis. Posting-level intervals are narrower than every skill-list interval in the grid ($[0.50, 0.55]$ against $[0.42, 0.63]$ at $P = 100$), so market-side sampling noise is not a competing explanation. We therefore report all SGI values with their $(K, P)$ settings and with corpus-specific confidence intervals rather than as universal constants; the earlier vocabulary-composition explanation of the level shift is withdrawn in favour of this measured profile-size sensitivity.

\subsection{RQ3: Domain-Level Skill Gap Quantification}

For each of the eight taxonomy domains we quantify: (i) \textit{market demand}---the share of postings whose skill set requires at least one taxonomy skill of the category; (ii) \textit{coverage rate}---the occurrence-weighted share of those category-skill requirements present in the occupational top-500 reference profile, evaluated by exact match and by dense matching at $\tau = 0.5$; and (iii) the resulting \textit{deficit} in percentage points of postings, $\text{deficit} = \text{demand} \times (1 - \text{coverage})$. Confidence intervals come from a 1,000-fold non-parametric bootstrap over postings (seed 42; experiment ALIGN03). Because the posting corpus is dominated by generic and non-technical competencies (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), absolute taxonomy deficits are small in magnitude. Each is therefore reported as an effect size in percentage points alongside its 95\% bootstrap interval, and no categorical severity label is assigned: on a 0--2.45 pp scale there is no externally anchored threshold at which one deficit becomes severe and another does not, so a relative-to-median label would encode the anchor rather than the measurement (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

\textbf{Check verdicts.} Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses} summarizes the three pre-specified checks derived in Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}, which also states why they are of unequal severity: H1 tests the reference profile rather than curricula, and H3 is near-guaranteed by construction. H1, a constructive-alignment prediction about curricular coverage, is \textit{consistent with} the measurements on the primary corpus and persists on the nested LinkedIn superset (one scrape family): technical categories carry the only nonzero reference-profile deficits while soft skills are fully covered. Because the instrument measures a posting-derived profile rather than curricula, we report consistency, not confirmation of the curricular claim. H2 (terminology divergence) is supported by the primary ALIGN01/02 contrast ($0.52$ vs.\ $0.32$), echoed within the same scrape family by the nested ALIGN04 check ($0.37$ vs.\ $0.14$), and consistent with the curricular-vocabulary measurement of CURR01 on a computational embedding match (lexical $0$--$2.4\%$ vs.\ semantic $30.4\%$)---and H3 (instrument robustness) is supported by strictly positive SGI and magnitude persistence on the nested LinkedIn superset (the lexical-above-semantic ordering is retained only as a sanity check, since every exact string match is also a cosine match of $1.0 \ge \tau$ by construction), and, at equal reference-profile covering-set size, identically on both nested samples of the scrape family---an identity that is arithmetic (the two corpora' top-$K$ market skill lists coincide exactly for $K \le 200$) and so supports H3 only weakly. \textbf{1) Terminology Divergence and Semantic Alignment:} Studies evaluating curriculum alignment through exact keyword matching often report misalignment rates exceeding 50\% \cite{baral2026analyzing}. Our findings qualify this observation. While exact string matching yields an apparent gap of 52.0\%, dense vector embeddings reduce the estimated deficit to 32.0\% at $\tau = 0.5$ (95\% CI: [23.0\%, 41.0\%]). This divergence indicates that a portion of the measured gap arises from vocabulary differences: occupational reference profiles and curricular descriptors often use conceptual terms (e.g., interpersonal communication or database principles), whereas job postings emphasize specific tool names or operational tasks. That expert-annotated lexical agreement is near chance ($\kappa = 0.112$; VALID01) while semantic agreement is substantial ($\kappa = 0.737$) shows this is a measurement property of keyword methods, not a modeling preference.

\subsection{Institutional Factors: One Interpretation of the Measured Deficits}

The persistent 32.0\% semantic deficit, and the domain-level deficits concentrated in Cloud Infrastructure (2.45 pp lexical; 0.94 pp semantic) and Programming \& Development (1.69 pp lexical), are consistent with---though do not by themselves establish---institutional constraints in curriculum adaptation \cite{biggs1996enhancing}, reported here as an interpretation of the measurements rather than as a finding:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Curriculum Governance and Accreditation:} Formal degree reviews and accreditation bodies operate on multi-year cycles (typically 3 to 6 years)---a documented institutional mechanism offered as an interpretation of the measurements. Modifying course requirements or introducing new technical electives requires administrative and faculty committee approvals that may lag industry tool adoption.
    \item \textbf{Faculty Development Latency:} Teaching rapidly evolving frameworks (e.g., container orchestration, distributed cloud platforms, specific ML toolchains) requires ongoing technical training, which institutional development budgets rarely prioritize over core research obligations.
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure and Computing Costs:} A candidate explanation this study does not test is that maintaining cloud accounts, GPU-capable compute, and industry-grade data environments imposes financial and operational constraints on computer science and engineering departments; establishing it would require cost and infrastructure evidence, which this sensitivity audit does not collect.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implications of the Measurement Results}

The measurements support claims about the instrument and the corpus---which vocabularies an exact matcher can and cannot resolve, how much of the posting-derived skill space the eight-category taxonomy covers, and how far a posting-derived reference profile sits from a technology inventory. They support no claim about curricula: no curriculum was read at course level and no institution is observed acting on any measurement, so this study derives no recommendation about course design, assessment practice, or accreditation. Four open questions follow from the measurements, and this study cannot answer any of them. Whether the 44.1\% lexical coverage of Cloud Infrastructure reflects curriculum content or the vocabulary of a keyword matcher applied to posting text would require course-level reading and human annotation of curricula. Whether the concentration of non-zero deficits in technical categories is an effect of corpus composition---the posting corpus is dominated by generic and non-technical competencies---rather than of curriculum content is untested here. Whether the deficits persist outside this single scrape family, and outside a corpus that is 85.2\% United States, is bounded only by the nested-superset check of ALIGN04, which is a sample-size extension within the same family. Whether a different taxonomy or a different covering-set size $P$ would move the deficits as much as the matcher threshold does is likewise unmeasured. These are recorded as open questions rather than as implications for practice, which is the scope Contribution~5 states.

\subsection{Reporting Checklist for Skill Gap Measurements}

\label{sec:reporting}

An SGI value is not interpretable without the settings that produced it, so every report should carry five things.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Configuration:} $K$, $P$ and $n_{\text{post}}$, and whether $P$ is the full profile or a top-$P$ covering set.

\item \textbf{Sensitivity range:} its place within the registered envelope (lexical $0.29$--$0.73$, semantic $\tau = 0.5$ $0.04$--$0.42$), not the value alone.

\item \textbf{Matched settings:} a cross-corpus or cross-experiment comparison is valid only at equal $(K, P)$; the covering-set sweep alone moves semantic SGI from $0.32$ to $0.04$.

\item \textbf{Matcher base rate:} the positive rate of the matcher's validation set ($39.1\%$ here); precision is prevalence-dependent, so the reported $91.9\%$ is an upper bound on deployment.

\item \textbf{Reference-profile provenance:} what it derives from and how many postings it rests on ($1{,}167$, posting-derived), and whether it is a curriculum sample (it is not).

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several methodological constraints should be considered when interpreting these findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Provenance \& Benchmark Artifacts:} While Tier 1 datasets contribute 1.35M unique web-mined postings (the HuggingFace Skill Gap tables are a strict subset of the LinkedIn scrape rather than a second sample; Section~\ref{sec:align04}), the Tier 2 student benchmarks are of unverified provenance (moderate-to-none generator signatures; \texttt{datasets/synthetic\_checks\_output.json}) and are excluded from the gap findings here; their provenance, decision-boundary, and transfer audit is reported in the companion benchmark article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}). The occupational reference profile is drawn from 1,167 job postings in five occupational categories rather than from institutionally collected syllabi.
    \item \textbf{Curriculum Proxy Limitations:} Academic skill demands were proxied through normalized occupational and educational reference profiles rather than direct, institutionally mined university syllabi across diverse global departments. Experiment CURR01 anchors this proxy to the CS2023 Body of Knowledge \cite{taskforce2024cs2023}, quantifying both its validity (30.4\% topical coverage, 49.6\% of profile skills grounded in the guidelines) and its bound. The anchor is a computational embedding-coverage measure---no human line-by-line topic audit was performed---so neither figure converts into a curricular quantity. Coverage is concentrated in applied Knowledge Areas (HCI, Data Management, Software Engineering) and weak over the theoretical core (Algorithmic Foundations 12.6\%, Mathematical Foundations 10.0\%), so conclusions about applied skill coverage are anchored while claims about the full curricular core are not.
    \item \textbf{Reference Profile Is Not a Curriculum Sample:} Every deficit reported here is the difference between a market corpus and a posting-derived reference profile. No university syllabus, course document or programme specification was collected or analysed, so these measurements do not establish the size---or the existence---of a curricular gap; CURR01 bounds the profile against CS2023 but does not convert the result into a curricular quantity.
    \item \textbf{Scope of Human Validation:} Matcher validity is established against published expert gold standards (SkillSpan$\to$ESCO mappings; $\kappa = 0.737$ at $\tau = 0.5$; Section~\ref{sec:valid01}) rather than in-house annotation of this specific corpus, and instrument robustness is supported by a nested-superset check (Section~\ref{sec:align04}); independent-rater annotation of the present skill pairs remains future work.
    \item \textbf{Tech-Only Stratification Is Registered but Pending (ALIGN05):} The corpus spans general occupations, so every deficit here is conditioned on taxonomy-technical demand shares that are small by construction (item 8 below). A pre-registered re-analysis restricted to computing and IT job titles (ALIGN05) would separate that conditioning from the measurement, and its design is already frozen in \texttt{experiments/ALIGN05\_PREREG.md}: a stratified 200-title sample scored against a sealed key, gated on design-weighted tech precision and recall each at $\geq 0.90$. It has not been run, because the human labels that gate it are unfilled (0 of 200 as of 2026-10-14) and the pre-registered gate stops the pipeline before any experiment executes. No computing-only SGI, precision or recall figure is reported, and none may be until the gate clears or a waiver is recorded with its disclosure.
    \item \textbf{Embedding Model and Threshold Sensitivity:} While Sentence-BERT mitigates surface vocabulary divergence, estimated alignment remains sensitive to cosine similarity cutoff selection ($\text{SGI} = 20.0\%$ at $\tau = 0.4$ versus $46.0\%$ at $\tau = 0.7$) and to reference-profile covering-set size ($P$): the matched-profile sensitivity in Section~\ref{sec:align04} shows SGI levels shifting from $0.52/0.32$ ($P = 100$) to $0.37/0.14$ ($P = 500$) on both nested corpora of the scrape family, so cross-experiment SGI comparisons are valid only at equal $(K, P)$. The same effect is visible between instruments: SENS02's skill-list interval for the identical $P = 100$, $\tau = 0.5$ cell is $[0.23, 0.42]$ against the headline $[0.23, 0.41]$---identical coverage ($0.68$) and point estimate ($0.32$), but SENS02 embeds a smaller string set, and a changed batch composition shifts a few cosines at the limit of float precision, reordering which top-100 skills sit exactly on the $\tau = 0.5$ boundary; because the bootstrap resamples coverage indicators by position, that tie-boundary reordering alone moves the interval's upper end.
    \item \textbf{Geographic Concentration:} 85.2\% of the LinkedIn posting corpus originates from the United States, skewing technical demand profiles toward Western, mature tech economies.
    \item \textbf{Corpus Composition \& Conditional Demand:} The 519K-posting skills corpus is dominated by generic, healthcare, and retail competencies (e.g., BLS/acls credentials and nursing appear among the Top-100 market tokens). Consequently, taxonomy-technical demand shares are modest (e.g., 3.97\% of postings require at least one Programming skill), and the RQ3 deficits should be read as \textit{coverage} gaps conditional on demand rather than as economy-wide demand shares. The nested LinkedIn check (ALIGN04) shows both sides of this conditioning: deficits shrink further on free-text skills (Programming 1.39 pp), and exact keyword taxonomy matching under-detects demand in corpora whose skill strings are not pre-normalized---so comparisons across the two annotation layers are valid for the \textit{ordering} of deficits but not their absolute magnitudes.
    \item \textbf{No External Anchor for Severity:} Absolute domain deficits are small (0.00--2.45 pp lexical) across eight fixed taxonomy categories, so no externally anchored threshold exists at which one deficit counts as severe and another does not. We therefore assign no categorical severity labels: each deficit is reported as an effect size in percentage points with its 95\% bootstrap interval in Table~\ref{tab:gap} and Figure~\ref{fig:skill_gap}, and any judgment about which magnitude matters must supply its own anchor. The ranking of categories by deficit is invariant to that choice.
\item \textbf{Overlap Detection Relies on Title Matching:} The reference profile carries no shared posting identifier with the market corpus, so the posting-level overlap check must fall back to normalized-title matching, which is a necessary rather than sufficient condition: generic titles such as ``Human Resources Manager'' recur hundreds of times in the market corpus, so title agreement alone is not evidence of a shared posting and the check requires skill-set corroboration. A duplicate whose title happened to differ from every profile title would be invisible to that join; to bound this, a title-free exact set-similarity search over per-posting skill sets ($1{,}167$ profile postings against $515{,}996$ distinct market skill sets) finds a maximum Jaccard of $0.667$ and no pair at $\geq 0.7$, so undetected skill-level duplication does not appear to be material. The overlap estimate is nonetheless a bound under a title-based join rather than a direct identifier match. Scope: the title-free search covered the primary $519{,}814$-posting market corpus ($518{,}953$ postings carrying skills); the $1{,}294{,}296$-posting superset used by ALIGN04 was not searched, so the $0.667$ bound attaches to the corpus behind the headline indices, not to the robustness superset.
    \item \textbf{The Demand-Side Check Is Not an Independent Confirmation (ALIGN06):} The supplementary O\*NET~$\times$ BLS check (Section~\ref{sec:align06}) must not be read as corroborating the gap. Skill-level SGI under this pairing is vocabulary-dominated: the demand and reference vocabularies share 90 of 8,613 O\*NET terms (1.0\%) and top-$K$ Jaccard against the posting corpora is $0.000$--$0.012$, so a positive SGI is close to guaranteed and its level ($0.97/0.68$) is not comparable to the posting-based $0.52/0.32$. The demand side is employment-weighted occupations rather than postings. Aggregating onto the eight taxonomy categories does not repair this either: the technology inventory maps no demand weight to Soft Skills, Business \& Domain or Cybersecurity (0.00 pp each, against 52.26, 11.81 and 0.62 pp in postings), the largest category differs across sources (Programming \& Development 21.67 pp in O\*NET against Cloud \& Infrastructure at a 2.45 pp lexical deficit in postings), and 39.1\% of employment-weighted demand falls outside the taxonomy, so category-level results are also not comparable across demand sources. No headline figure is revised from ALIGN06.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On the tiered corpus described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} (1.35M real web-mined postings, with 316K unverified aggregate rows and 458K unverified-provenance benchmark rows recorded but excluded from these findings), this empirical investigation---which proxies academic skill requirements with an employer-derived occupational reference profile rather than university syllabi---shows that profile--market misalignment is twofold. A substantial share of the apparent deficit reflects vocabulary divergence between reference-profile descriptors and recruitment postings (the SGI falls from 52\% to 32\% under semantic alignment, and several domain deficits shrink by more than half), while substantive residual reference-profile deficits remain concentrated in rapidly evolving technical areas, specifically Cloud Infrastructure and Programming \& Development. Whether those residuals correspond to curricular shortfalls is not established by this study. The instrument supporting this claim is examined three ways: expert-gold agreement on published ESCO mappings ($\kappa = 0.737$), a nested-superset robustness check on 1.29M postings from the same scrape family (both indices strictly positive; identical SGI at matched reference-profile covering-set size), and a computational anchor to the CS2023 curricular guidelines (embedding coverage concentrated in applied knowledge areas; no human line-by-line topic audit was performed). If the residual reference-profile deficits do correspond to curricular shortfalls, addressing them would not necessitate wholesale curriculum redesign---an inference we mark as conditional and do not test here. Because the measurements cannot by themselves separate vocabulary divergence from curricular shortfall, the next step is a study that measures curricula directly; the modular-credential and assessment options in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} remain candidate responses conditional on such evidence.
