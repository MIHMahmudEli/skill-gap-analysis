% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Disparities between higher education curricula and labor-market skill demands are widely reported, yet large-scale empirical studies that link hiring requirements with what curricula actually teach remain limited. We examine academic--industry alignment using 15 open datasets (2.12M unique records after removing linked-table and nested-corpus duplication) collected from LinkedIn, HuggingFace, and public benchmark repositories. Academic skill requirements are proxied by an employer-derived occupational reference profile (1,167 job postings across five occupational categories) rather than by institutionally collected university syllabi. Grounded in Human Capital Theory, Job Market Signaling, and Constructive Alignment, the analysis combines: (1) n-gram skill extraction across an eight-category taxonomy; (2) semantic vector alignment via Sentence-BERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) evaluated across four cosine similarity thresholds ($\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$) with 1,000-fold bootstrap confidence intervals. Exact string matching yields an apparent Skill Gap Index (SGI) of 52.0\%. Dense vector alignment reduces this estimated gap to 32.0\% at baseline threshold $\tau = 0.5$ (95\% bootstrap CI: [23.0\%, 41.0\%]), rising to 46.0\% (95\% CI: [37.0\%, 56.0\%]) under a stricter criterion ($\tau = 0.7$). Deficits concentrate primarily in emerging technical areas: domain-level decomposition localizes residual reference-profile deficits in Cloud Infrastructure (2.45 percentage points at lexical matching, 0.94 pp after semantic alignment) and Programming \& Development (1.69 pp lexical), while soft-skill requirements are fully covered by the academic reference profile. The measurement instrument is examined three ways: agreement with published expert ESCO mappings ($\kappa = 0.737$ at $\tau = 0.5$, ROC-AUC $0.961$), a re-check of both gap indices on a nested LinkedIn superset drawn from the same scrape family (1.29M postings; lexical/semantic SGI $37\%/14\%$ versus $52\%/32\%$, preserving the ordering of instruments and thresholds), and a computational---not human-validated---anchor of the academic reference profile to the ACM/IEEE-CS/AAAI CS2023 curriculum Body of Knowledge via embedding coverage (2,197 topics across 17 knowledge areas; $30.4\%$ semantic coverage, concentrated in applied areas). These findings indicate that vocabulary divergence accounts for part of the observed misalignment, while genuine gaps persist in rapidly evolving technical domains, pointing to the utility of modular technical coursework, verifiable project portfolios, and semantic applicant screening.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{echnological} developments across distributed cloud architectures, data engineering, and artificial intelligence have altered employer skill requirements across major economic sectors. Because university curricula are updated through multi-year institutional review cycles, degree programs often adapt more slowly than market demands evolve \cite{jaiswal2024understanding, jumaan2026understanding}. Educational researchers and labor economists refer to this discrepancy as the \textit{skills gap}---the divergence between competencies taught in degree programs and the practical proficiencies sought by employers \cite{febriani2025employability, barachino2025operationalization}.

Reports from the World Economic Forum \cite{wef2020} indicate that over 40\% of core worker skills are expected to shift within five-year windows, particularly in artificial intelligence, cloud architectures, and data engineering. In addition to specialized technical skills, employer surveys regularly report deficits in transferable competencies such as professional communication and collaborative problem-solving \cite{tight2023employability, braun2026concept, noor2024skills}. 

Prior empirical research on curriculum--market alignment exhibits four main limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{Limited Sample Scope:} Most studies rely on institutional surveys, small employer samples ($N < 500$), or single-nation case studies \cite{baral2026analyzing, alhazmi2025aligning}, offering limited visibility into cross-national patterns.
    \item \textbf{Methodological Separation:} Investigations typically employ qualitative coding \cite{rikala2024understanding} or predictive classification without semantic text analysis, leaving unresolved whether reported gaps reflect distinct vocabularies or absent skills \cite{kruger2025improving, lai2025learning}.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Relevance:} Several widely cited baseline studies pre-date recent enterprise developments, including widespread cloud migration and the deployment of generative AI tools (2022--2026) \cite{anthuvan2026reverse, sun2025creative}.
    \item \textbf{Limited Theoretical Grounding:} Computational employability models are frequently reported without reference to economic or educational frameworks that explain curriculum lag \cite{schlippe2023skill, eimer2023employability}.
\end{enumerate}

To address these questions, we analyze 15 open datasets (2.12M unique records; seven sources feed the experiments reported here) from LinkedIn, HuggingFace, and public benchmark repositories across three research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Market Topography):} What are the most frequent skill requirements in online job postings?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Lexical versus Semantic Alignment):} To what degree do academic reference-profile descriptors align with market requirements when evaluated using exact string matching versus dense semantic vector embeddings?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Domain-Specific Deficits):} What is the magnitude of the skill deficit across distinct subject areas (Programming, Data Science, Cloud Infrastructure, Soft Skills, Cybersecurity)?
\end{itemize}

Placement-benchmark analyses (employability determinants and cross-cohort placement dynamics; experiments STAT01, STAT02, PLAC01, INTL01) are reported in the companion benchmark-audit article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}); the geographic distribution of postings (GEO01) is reported within RQ1 above.

\textbf{Contributions:} This work makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Harmonized Multi-Source Corpus:} We assemble 15 open datasets---1.35M real web-mined job postings, a posting-derived academic reference profile (1,167 postings), 316K unverified aggregated title--skill rows, and 458K benchmark rows of unverified provenance (including 421K student/alumni records; evidence tiers from strong signatures to none found, plus one file declared synthetic by its source, are recorded in \texttt{datasets/REAL\_VS\_SYNTHETIC.csv}).
    \item \textbf{Dual Lexical--Semantic Analysis:} Using Sentence-BERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) alongside exact string matching, we show that exact matching yields a 52.0\% gap, whereas semantic vector alignment reduces this estimate to 32.0\% at $\tau = 0.5$.
    \item \textbf{Externally Validated Measurement Instrument:} Beyond in-sample estimates, we validate the matcher against published expert ESCO gold mappings ($\kappa = 0.737$) and re-check both gap indices on a nested-superset robustness sample of 1.29M postings from the same scrape family (a sample-size extension, not an independent replication); a separate computational anchor (embedding coverage, not human audit) relates the academic reference profile to the CS2023 curricular Body of Knowledge.
    \item \textbf{Threshold Sensitivity Estimation:} We compute 1,000-fold bootstrap confidence intervals across four similarity thresholds ($\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$) to evaluate how threshold selection affects gap measurement.
    \item \textbf{Theory-Informed Recommendations:} Drawing on Human Capital Theory, Signaling Theory, and Constructive Alignment, we outline practical adjustments for course design, project evaluation, and recruitment screening, accompanied by explicit documentation of dataset provenance and limitations.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this article is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work and outlines the theoretical framework. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the datasets, text processing pipeline, and statistical methods. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical results for RQ1--RQ3. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses theoretical and institutional implications, practical recommendations, reproducibility, and threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the study. The placement-model experiments (STAT01, STAT02, PLAC01, INTL01) are audited in a companion article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}).

\section{Related Work and Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Conceptualizations of the Skills Gap}

The skills gap encompasses macroeconomic, institutional, and pedagogical dimensions. Conceptualizations differ substantially across stakeholders: industry surveys often define gaps in terms of immediate job-readiness, whereas university leaders emphasize broad intellectual development and foundational theory \cite{braun2026concept, tight2023employability}. Digital transformation has also increased demand for cross-disciplinary competencies, such as data literacy among non-computing professionals \cite{rikala2024understanding}.

Computational curriculum analytics has sought to quantify these dynamics using course descriptions and accreditation criteria. Cuckov et al. \cite{cuckov2022quantifying} evaluated computer engineering courses against ABET criteria, finding delays in updating upper-level electives. Zhao et al. \cite{zhao2026proximity} measured structural proximity between academic research and patent descriptions using knowledge graphs. Focusing on software engineering, Korrapati et al. \cite{korrapati2025aligning} and Alhazmi et al. \cite{alhazmi2025aligning} examined curriculum alignment with cloud computing and AI requirements, noting that elective coursework often trails industry tool adoption.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}

Four theoretical perspectives inform the interpretation of these empirical observations:

\textbf{1) Human Capital Theory (HCT):} Formulated by Becker \cite{becker1964human} and Schultz \cite{schultz1961investment}, HCT posits that education enhances productive capacity, yielding labor-market returns. HCT distinguishes between \textit{general human capital} (transferable competencies such as critical thinking, communication, and core mathematical reasoning) and \textit{firm-specific human capital} (proficiency with specialized software tools, proprietary APIs, or operational frameworks). Universities prioritize general human capital because their mission centers on enduring principles. Conversely, employer job descriptions frequently specify immediately applicable technical tools. This distinction helps explain why measured reference-profile deficits concentrate in applied toolchains such as cloud platforms and data libraries.

\textbf{2) Job Market Signaling and Screening Theory:} Spence \cite{spence1973job} established that in labor markets characterized by asymmetric information, observable artifacts serve as informational signals that reduce hiring risk. In technical recruitment, degree credentials are increasingly complemented by practical capability signals such as project portfolios and open-source contributions \cite{pianda2024impact, scandurra2024employability}---the portfolio-signal idea that grounds our recommendation for verifiable applied project work below. (The placement-determinant tests of this theory are reported in the companion benchmark audit, \texttt{paper\_placement/}.)

\textbf{3) Constructive Alignment \& Curriculum Theory:} Biggs \cite{biggs1996enhancing} introduced constructive alignment to establish coherence among Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs), teaching methods, and assessment tasks. At an institutional level, curriculum lag can be understood as \textit{external misalignment}: ILOs are governed by multi-year accreditation reviews (such as ABET and ACM/IEEE guidelines), whereas industry skill demands evolve on shorter market cycles.

\textbf{4) Skill-Biased Technological Change (SBTC):} Acemoglu and Autor \cite{acemoglu2011skills} formalized SBTC to explain how technological progress augments the productivity of workers performing non-routine cognitive tasks while reducing demand for routine labor. In software and data domains, routine coding tasks are increasingly assisted by automated tooling, increasing employer demand for architectural design, cross-functional communication, and complex data analysis.

\subsection{Natural Language Processing for Curriculum Analytics}

Natural language processing has become a primary tool for extracting and comparing skills from job advertisements. Skill identification from postings is itself an established research program, survey-driven since Khaouja et al. \cite{khaouja2021survey}, and evaluated against human-annotated benchmark corpora whose annotation schemes distinguish skills from qualifications, experience, and occupation \cite{green2022development, zhang2022skillspan}. Recent deep-learning surveys catalogue the datasets and the persistent inconsistency in hard/soft-skill terminology that motivates semantic over lexical comparison \cite{senger2024deep}. While keyword extraction is widely used, rule-based matching often fails on nuanced occupational phrases \cite{kruger2025improving}. Pre-trained language representations address this limitation by projecting terms into continuous vector spaces. For instance, Baral and Ghimire \cite{baral2026analyzing} used embeddings to measure syllabus alignment in Nepal, showing that semantic representations bridge terminology differences between academic syllabi and job listings. Text-mining methods have similarly been applied to profile data science roles \cite{mol2024unravelling, tee2024demand} and build resume-matching systems \cite{daberao2025resumeinsight}. In this work, we use Sentence-BERT (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) \cite{reimers2019sentence} to quantify alignment between market requirements and academic descriptors across multiple similarity thresholds, and we validate the resulting matcher against expert mappings rather than assuming its accuracy.

\subsection{Testable Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The four theoretical perspectives of Section~\ref{sec:theory} yield three testable predictions, each evaluated by at least one registered experiment (Table~\ref{tab:experiments}):
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*:}]
    \item \textbf{Constructive Alignment (external misalignment):} Because Intended Learning Outcomes are governed by accreditation cycles while toolchains evolve continuously, curricular coverage should be complete for enduring general competencies (soft skills) but incomplete for applied technical toolchains (Cloud, Programming, Data Science) \cite{biggs1996enhancing}. \textit{Test:} domain deficits in ALIGN03 and their nested-superset re-check in ALIGN04.
    \item \textbf{Terminology divergence (measurement):} If a share of the reported gap is vocabulary mismatch rather than absent competence \cite{kruger2025improving}, dense semantic matching should remove a substantial share of the lexically measured gap, and the matches it adds beyond exact string matching should be predominantly valid. Because every exact string match is also a cosine match at $\tau \le 0.7$, the \emph{direction} of the change is guaranteed by construction; the hypotheses concern the \emph{magnitude} of the reduction (with bootstrap confidence intervals) and the validity of the added matches. \textit{Tests:} ALIGN01 vs.\ ALIGN02 (magnitude of reduction); ALIGN04 (nested superset) and CURR01 (a third vocabulary) for the same question; VALID01 (expert gold) for the validity of the additional matches.
    \item \textbf{Instrument robustness:} If the Skill Gap Index measures a property of the profile--market pair rather than of one sample of it, both variants of SGI should remain strictly positive and the magnitude of the semantic reduction should persist when the market sample is extended from the 519K-posting primary corpus to a nested superset of the same posting-scrape family. \textit{Test:} ALIGN04 (LinkedIn nested superset, $N = 1.29$M postings).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Data Corpus and Provenance Architecture}

The corpus comprises 15 open datasets acquired in September 2026 from Kaggle and HuggingFace repositories under open-access licenses (CC0, CC-BY 4.0, and ODbL). Sources carry per-source observation and release windows listed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}; the regional profiles are calibrated against the India Skills Report 2026 (that source declares itself synthetic). Summing raw file row counts yields 3,163,517 records; a provenance audit (\texttt{datasets/OVERLAP\_AUDIT.csv}) then shows that the two HuggingFace Skill Gap tables share an identical set of 519,814 \texttt{job\_link} identifiers (a linked pair, not two samples) and that this entire pair is a strict subset of the LinkedIn 2024 postings table (all 519,814 links present), de-duplicating to 2,123,889 unique record rows. That total pools four incompatible row units and is therefore not used as an all-purpose evidence count anywhere in this paper: 1,349,621 real web-scraped posting rows (the 1,348,454 LinkedIn postings plus a 1,167-posting posting-derived academic-reference subsample; the 519,814 nested HuggingFace postings contribute no additional rows), 519,814 posting--skill pair rows that vanish on de-duplication, 316,279 aggregated title--skill count rows of unverifiable provenance, and 457,989 benchmark rows of unverified provenance (445,989 signature-flagged---a 10,345-row AI job series with strong generator signatures, a 15,000-row AI salary file with moderate signatures, and 420,644 student/alumni records with moderate-to-weak signatures---plus 12,000 India-skill profiles declared synthetic by their source; evidence tiers are computed from the share of applicable discriminating checks fired, in \texttt{datasets/REAL\_VS\_SYNTHETIC.csv} and \texttt{datasets/synthetic\_checks\_output.json}). Table~\ref{tab:datasets} reports the same accounting grouped by row unit. The academic-reference file contributes 1,167 job postings rather than a separate 13,833-row taxonomy. Seven of the 15 sources feed the experiments reported below; the remainder (including the generated AI-trends series, retained in the registry but not used here) are retained for inventory completeness (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the sources, sample sizes, observation windows, and licenses.

To avoid pooling datasets with incompatible collection mechanisms, we divide the corpus into two functional tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Online Labor Market Postings (1.35M real web-scraped postings; 316K unverified aggregate rows; the linked HuggingFace pair contributes no unique rows):} Comprises the LinkedIn dataset (1.35M records) \cite{asaniczka2024linkedin}, the USA Job Market dataset (316K records) \cite{batuhanklac2024usa}, and the linked HuggingFace Skill Gap jobs--skills pair (519K postings with skill annotations), which the provenance audit shows to be a strict subset of the LinkedIn postings table and which therefore contributes no additional unique records. These corpora contain job advertisements from North America, Europe, and the Asia-Pacific region, providing direct observations of employer skill specifications.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: Student Placement and Career Benchmarks ($N \approx 421\text{K}$ student/alumni benchmark rows of unverified provenance, moderate-to-none generator signatures; no verified real graduate records):} Comprises College Student Placement ($N = 10,000$) \cite{sumedh15072024college}, NGESS Graduate Employment ($N = 7,200$) \cite{philipdayo2024ngess}, Global Student Placement ($N = 10,000$) \cite{rakesh6302024global}, Student Career Success ($N = 50,000$) \cite{mobeenfatimah2024career}, International Graduates ($N = 300,000$) \cite{quackquackrp2024international}, Field-of-Study vs.\ Occupation ($N = 38,444$) \cite{jahnavipaliwal2024field}, and India Fresher Market ($N = 5,000$) \cite{shambhurajejagadale2024india} ($420{,}644$ records in total). These tabular records provide academic, behavioral, and placement indicators; the placement-benchmark experiments built on them are audited in the companion article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Taxonomy Construction and Skill Extraction}

We define an eight-category occupational skill taxonomy $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_8\}$ synthesized from the ACM/IEEE CS2023 curriculum guidelines and O*NET descriptors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Programming \& Development:} Python, Java, C++, JavaScript, SQL, TypeScript, Go, Rust, React, Docker, Kubernetes, Git.
    \item \textbf{Data Science \& Analytics:} Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Statistical Analysis, PyTorch, TensorFlow, NLP, Computer Vision.
    \item \textbf{Cloud \& Infrastructure:} AWS, Azure, GCP, CI/CD Pipelines, Terraform, Linux Administration, Serverless Architectures.
    \item \textbf{Soft Skills:} Professional Communication, Leadership, Teamwork, Critical Thinking, Time Management, Adaptability.
    \item \textbf{Business \& Domain:} Agile/Scrum, Product Management, Requirements Gathering, Business Analysis, Risk Compliance.
    \item \textbf{Design \& UX:} UI/UX Design, Figma, User Research, Wireframing, Prototyping, Usability Testing.
    \item \textbf{Data Engineering:} ETL Pipelines, Apache Airflow, Apache Kafka, Distributed Data Warehousing, PostgreSQL, NoSQL.
    \item \textbf{Cybersecurity:} Network Security, Penetration Testing, Incident Response, SIEM Systems, ISO 27001, Cryptography.
\end{enumerate}

Skill extraction from text is conducted using regularized n-gram tokenization matched against taxonomy dictionaries, as summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:skill_extract}. Academic skill requirements are represented using a standardized occupational--educational reference profile (job-facing skill lists from five occupational categories) rather than scraped syllabi---a proxy whose content validity is quantified against CS2023 in CURR01 (Section~\ref{sec:curr01}); profile-based matching without scraped syllabi follows established practice in semantic resume--job systems \cite{daberao2025resumeinsight}. Extraction and matching are fully automated, with matcher validity established externally against expert ESCO annotations rather than in-house re-annotation (Section~\ref{sec:valid01}).

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Semantic Skill Extraction and Embedding Alignment}
\label{alg:skill_extract}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Market text corpus $\mathcal{D}_M$, Academic reference-profile corpus $\mathcal{D}_A$, Taxonomy $\mathcal{C}$, Sentence-BERT model $\mathcal{E}$, Threshold $\tau$
\ENSURE Market skill set $\mathcal{M}$, Academic skill set $\mathcal{A}$, Similarity matrix $\mathbf{S}$, Gap Index $\text{SGI}(\tau)$
\STATE $\mathcal{M} \leftarrow \emptyset, \mathcal{A} \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FORALL{$d \in \mathcal{D}_M$}
    \STATE Extract normalized skill tokens $T_d \leftarrow \text{Tokenize}(d, \mathcal{C})$
    \STATE $\mathcal{M} \leftarrow \mathcal{M} \cup T_d$
\ENDFOR
\FORALL{$d \in \mathcal{D}_A$}
    \STATE Extract normalized reference-profile tokens $T_a \leftarrow \text{Tokenize}(d, \mathcal{C})$
    \STATE $\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \mathcal{A} \cup T_a$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Filter $\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}} \leftarrow \text{Top-}K(\mathcal{M}), \mathcal{A}_{\text{top}} \leftarrow \text{Top-}P(\mathcal{A})$ \COMMENT{$K{=}100$; $P{\in}\{100,500\}$ by experiment (Sec.~III)} 
\STATE Compute dense embeddings: $\mathbf{E}_M \leftarrow \mathcal{E}(\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}), \mathbf{E}_A \leftarrow \mathcal{E}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{top}})$
\STATE Compute pairwise cosine similarity:
\STATE $\mathbf{S}_{ij} \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf{e}_{m,i} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{a,j}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{m,i}\|_2 \|\mathbf{e}_{a,j}\|_2} \quad \forall m_i \in \mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}, a_j \in \mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}$
\FORALL{$i \in \{1, \dots, |\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}|\}$}
    \STATE $\sigma_i \leftarrow \max_j \mathbf{S}_{ij}$
    \STATE $\text{covered}_i \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(\sigma_i \ge \tau)$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\text{SGI}(\tau) \leftarrow 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}|} \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}|} \text{covered}_i$
\RETURN $\mathcal{M}_{\text{top}}, \mathcal{A}_{\text{top}}, \mathbf{S}, \text{SGI}(\tau)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Semantic Vector Alignment via Sentence-BERT}

Exact string matching can overestimate profile--market gaps when the academic reference profile and job postings use different wording for equivalent skills (for example, ``interpersonal communication'' versus ``client communication skills''). To account for semantic similarity, we use the Sentence-BERT model (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) \cite{reimers2019sentence}.

Let $\mathbf{e}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ denote the normalized dense vector embedding of market skill $s \in \mathcal{M}$, and let $\mathbf{e}_{s'} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ denote the embedding of academic reference-profile descriptor $s' \in \mathcal{A}$. The semantic alignment score is given by:
\begin{equation}
\text{sim}(s, s') = \frac{\mathbf{e}_s \cdot \mathbf{e}_{s'}}{\|\mathbf{e}_s\|_2 \|\mathbf{e}_{s'}\|_2}
\label{eq:cosine}
\end{equation}

A market skill $s$ is designated as covered at threshold $\tau \in [0, 1]$ if:
\begin{equation}
\text{Covered}(s; \tau) = \mathbb{I}\left(\max_{s' \in \mathcal{A}} \text{sim}(s, s') \ge \tau\right)
\label{eq:covered}
\end{equation}

The Skill Gap Index (SGI) at threshold $\tau$ is defined as the complement of the mean coverage rate across the market skill space:
\begin{equation}
\text{SGI}(\tau) = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{M}} \text{Covered}(s; \tau)
\label{eq:sgi_tau}
\end{equation}

To estimate empirical uncertainty without parametric distribution assumptions, we implement a 1,000-fold non-parametric bootstrap over the binary coverage indicator array with fixed random seed ($\text{seed} = 42$), computing 95\% percentile confidence intervals:
\begin{equation}
\text{CI}_{95\%} = \left[\hat{\theta}^*_{(0.025)}, \hat{\theta}^*_{(0.975)}\right]
\label{eq:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
We use $\tau = 0.5$ as the baseline threshold and evaluate sensitivity at $\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$. The market profile size is fixed at $K = 100$ throughout. The academic covering-set size is $P = 100$ for the headline SGI experiments (ALIGN01/02) and $P = 500$ for the domain-deficit and nested-superset experiments (ALIGN03/04) and CURR01; Section~\ref{sec:align04} reports a matched-profile sensitivity that shows how SGI level varies with $P$.

\subsection{Statistical Inference}

All interval estimates reported in this article are 95\% percentile intervals from the 1{,}000-fold non-parametric bootstrap defined in Equation~\ref{eq:bootstrap} (seed 42). Group-comparison statistics for the placement cohort (Welch's $t$-test, Cohen's $d$, Cram\'er's $V$, and variance-inflation diagnostics) are defined, executed, and reported in the companion benchmark-audit article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}), together with the classifier and decision-boundary diagnostics; they play no role in the alignment results reported here.

section's central finding: exact string matching understates true

instrument. A complementary vocabulary check against the 13,896-skill ESCO
inventory shows that only 6\% of the market Top-100 and 11\% of the
academic Top-100 appear verbatim in ESCO, yet dense matching at $\tau =
0.5$ resolves 91\% [85\%, 96\%] and 99\% [97\%, 100\%] of them to an ESCO
skill under paraphrase---evidence that the extracted vocabularies are
semantically grounded in an expert-curated taxonomy.

\subsubsection{Nested-Superset Robustness Check on LinkedIn (ALIGN04)}
\label{sec:align04}

A single-sample instrument invites the objection that the measured gap is an artefact of one draw from the market. A pairwise provenance audit shows that the primary 519,814-posting corpus is a strict subset of the LinkedIn 2024 scrape (every \texttt{job\_link} present), so Experiment ALIGN04 functions as a nested-superset robustness check---a sample-size extension within one scrape family, not an independent replication---by re-running the lexical and semantic procedures on the larger LinkedIn sample ($N = 1{,}294{,}296$ postings with skill annotations), with the academic covering set at $P = 500$ to match ALIGN03 (top-100 market profile, seed 42, 1,000-fold bootstrap). H2 and H3 receive direct tests under this extension. Exact matching yields SGI $= 0.37$ [0.28, 0.47] and dense matching at $\tau = 0.5$ yields SGI $= 0.14$ [0.07, 0.21]; both confidence intervals exclude zero, and the lexical-above-semantic ordering and the monotone increase with $\tau$ match the primary-corpus pattern (Table~\ref{tab:crosscorpus}).

\textbf{Profile-size sensitivity.} Because ALIGN01/02 use academic $P = 100$ while ALIGN03/04 use $P = 500$, the raw level contrast (HF $0.52/0.32$ vs.\ LinkedIn $0.37/0.14$) confounds corpus with covering-set size. We therefore re-estimate SGI on \emph{both} corpora at \emph{both} profile sizes (identical tokenizers, model, and seed): at $P = 100$, HF and LinkedIn both give lexical/semantic SGI $= 0.52/0.32$; at $P = 500$, both give $0.37/0.14$. The instrument is thus exactly stable across corpora at matched profile size---supporting H3 even more cleanly than a raw level comparison---while the \emph{level} of SGI is a property of the academic covering-set size, not of the market corpus. We therefore report all SGI values with their $(K, P)$ settings and with corpus-specific confidence intervals rather than as universal constants; the earlier vocabulary-composition explanation of the level shift is withdrawn in favour of this measured profile-size sensitivity.

\subsection{RQ3: Domain-Level Skill Gap Quantification}

For each of the eight taxonomy domains we quantify: (i) \textit{market demand}---the share of postings whose skill set requires at least one taxonomy skill of the category; (ii) \textit{coverage rate}---the occurrence-weighted share of those category-skill requirements present in the academic top-500 reference profile, evaluated by exact match and by dense matching at $\tau = 0.5$; and (iii) the resulting \textit{deficit} in percentage points of postings, $\text{deficit} = \text{demand} \times (1 - \text{coverage})$. Confidence intervals come from a 1,000-fold non-parametric bootstrap over postings (seed 42; experiment ALIGN03). Because the posting corpus is dominated by generic and non-technical competencies (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), absolute taxonomy deficits are small in magnitude; severity is therefore assigned \textit{relative to the cross-category median deficit} (Critical $\ge 2\times$ the median, Moderate $\ge$ median, Mild otherwise).

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

\textbf{Hypothesis verdicts.} Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses} summarizes the three predictions derived in Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}. H1 (constructive alignment) is supported on the primary corpus and persists on the nested LinkedIn superset (one scrape family): technical categories carry the only nonzero deficits while soft skills are fully covered. H2 (terminology divergence) is supported by the primary ALIGN01/02 contrast ($0.52$ vs.\ $0.32$), echoed within the same scrape family by the nested ALIGN04 check ($0.37$ vs.\ $0.14$), and independently attested on curricular vocabulary by CURR01 (lexical $0$--$2.4\%$ vs.\ semantic $30.4\%$)---and H3 (instrument robustness) is supported by strictly positive SGI and magnitude persistence on the nested LinkedIn superset (the lexical-above-semantic ordering is retained only as a sanity check, since every exact string match is also a cosine match of $1.0 \ge \tau$ by construction), and more strongly still by the matched-profile sensitivity showing identical SGI on both nested samples of the scrape family at equal academic covering-set size. \textbf{1) Terminology Divergence and Semantic Alignment:} Studies evaluating curriculum alignment through exact keyword matching often report misalignment rates exceeding 50\% \cite{baral2026analyzing}. Our findings qualify this observation. While exact string matching yields an apparent gap of 52.0\%, dense vector embeddings reduce the estimated deficit to 32.0\% at $\tau = 0.5$ (95\% CI: [23.0\%, 41.0\%]). This divergence indicates that a portion of the measured gap arises from vocabulary differences: academic reference profiles and curricular descriptors often use conceptual terms (e.g., interpersonal communication or database principles), whereas job postings emphasize specific tool names or operational tasks. That expert-annotated lexical agreement is near chance ($\kappa = 0.112$; VALID01) while semantic agreement is substantial ($\kappa = 0.737$) shows this is a measurement property of keyword methods, not a modeling preference.

\subsection{Institutional Factors in Curriculum Adaptation}

The persistent 32.0\% semantic deficit, and the domain-level deficits concentrated in Cloud Infrastructure (2.45 pp lexical; 0.94 pp semantic) and Programming \& Development (1.69 pp lexical), are consistent with---though do not by themselves establish---institutional constraints in curriculum adaptation \cite{biggs1996enhancing}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Curriculum Governance and Accreditation:} Formal degree reviews and accreditation bodies operate on multi-year cycles (typically 3 to 6 years). Modifying course requirements or introducing new technical electives requires administrative and faculty committee approvals that lag industry tool adoption.
    \item \textbf{Faculty Development Latency:} Teaching rapidly evolving frameworks (e.g., container orchestration, distributed cloud platforms, specific ML toolchains) requires ongoing technical training, which institutional development budgets rarely prioritize over core research obligations.
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure and Computing Costs:} Maintaining cloud accounts, GPU resources, and industry-grade data environments presents financial and operational hurdles for many computer science and engineering departments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implications and Recommendations}

We organize recommendations for higher education, recruitment, and accreditation around the empirical findings:

\textbf{For Higher Education Institutions (HEIs):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Integration of Modular Technical Credentials:} Cloud Infrastructure has the lowest reference-profile coverage of any category (44.1\% lexical; 78.7\% under semantic matching) and is the only Critical deficit, while Programming \& Development retains a 1.69 pp deficit. Full degree restructuring may be too slow to keep pace with industry toolchains; HEIs can address this by embedding modular, vendor-neutral technical modules (e.g., containerization fundamentals, cloud storage basics) directly into existing operating systems and database courses.
    \item \textit{Applied Project Work and Portfolio Development:} Because completed project work is the most portable evidence of applied technical ability, curricula should prioritize multi-term capstone projects that require version control, system deployment, and written documentation, giving students verifiable portfolios rather than relying on credentials alone (the portfolio-signal rationale of Section~\ref{sec:related_work}).
    \item \textit{Embedding Communication Skills into Technical Coursework:} The academic reference profile already covers soft-skill requirements in full (deficit $0.00$ pp). The priority is therefore not new content but assessment: rather than relegating communication instruction to standalone electives, departments should integrate oral presentations, collaborative design reviews, and peer code reviews directly into core computing courses.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{For Industry Recruiters:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Semantic Alignment in Recruitment Screening:} Because exact string matching produced a 52.0\% gap compared with 32.0\% under semantic alignment, keyword-based resume screening risks excluding candidates with equivalent conceptual training. Recruitment systems should evaluate semantic similarity rather than exact keyword matches when parsing academic coursework.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{For Accreditation Bodies and Policymakers:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Adaptive Curriculum Guidelines:} Foundational requirements (Python, SQL) recur at similar shares in every corpus view in this study while specialized frameworks diversify across postings, a pattern consistent with rapid toolchain turnover. Accreditation bodies and professional societies (ACM, IEEE-CS) should issue regular modular addenda to curriculum guidelines to facilitate elective updates.
    \item \textit{Regional Labor-Market Observatories:} Given the concentration of postings (85.2\% US), educational authorities should maintain regional labor-market tracking to help institutions align elective offerings with local economic demand.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several methodological constraints should be considered when interpreting these findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Provenance \& Benchmark Artifacts:} While Tier 1 datasets contribute 1.35M unique web-mined postings (the HuggingFace Skill Gap tables are a strict subset of the LinkedIn scrape rather than a second sample; Section~\ref{sec:align04}), the Tier 2 student benchmarks are of unverified provenance (moderate-to-none generator signatures; \texttt{datasets/synthetic\_checks\_output.json}) and are excluded from the gap findings here; their provenance, decision-boundary, and transfer audit is reported in the companion benchmark article (\texttt{paper\_placement/}). The academic reference profile is drawn from 1,167 job postings in five occupational categories rather than from institutionally collected syllabi.
    \item \textbf{Curriculum Proxy Limitations:} Academic skill demands were proxied through normalized occupational and educational reference profiles rather than direct, institutionally mined university syllabi across diverse global departments. Experiment CURR01 anchors this proxy to the CS2023 Body of Knowledge \cite{taskforce2024cs2023}, quantifying both its validity (30.4\% topical coverage, 49.6\% of profile skills grounded in the guidelines) and its bound: coverage is concentrated in applied Knowledge Areas (HCI, Data Management, Software Engineering) and weak over the theoretical core (Algorithmic Foundations 12.6\%, Mathematical Foundations 10.0\%), so conclusions about applied skill coverage are anchored while claims about the full curricular core are not.
    \item \textbf{Scope of Human Validation:} Matcher validity is established against published expert gold standards (SkillSpan$\to$ESCO mappings; $\kappa = 0.737$ at $\tau = 0.5$; Section~\ref{sec:valid01}) rather than in-house annotation of this specific corpus, and instrument robustness is supported by a nested-superset check (Section~\ref{sec:align04}); independent-rater annotation of the present skill pairs remains future work.
    \item \textbf{Embedding Model and Threshold Sensitivity:} While Sentence-BERT mitigates surface vocabulary divergence, estimated alignment remains sensitive to cosine similarity cutoff selection ($\text{SGI} = 20.0\%$ at $\tau = 0.4$ versus $46.0\%$ at $\tau = 0.7$) and to academic covering-set size ($P$): the matched-profile sensitivity in Section~\ref{sec:align04} shows SGI levels shifting from $0.52/0.32$ ($P = 100$) to $0.37/0.14$ ($P = 500$) on both nested corpora of the scrape family, so cross-experiment SGI comparisons are valid only at equal $(K, P)$.
    \item \textbf{Geographic Concentration:} 85.2\% of the LinkedIn posting corpus originates from the United States, skewing technical demand profiles toward Western, mature tech economies.
    \item \textbf{Corpus Composition \& Conditional Demand:} The 519K-posting skills corpus is dominated by generic, healthcare, and retail competencies (e.g., BLS/acls credentials and nursing appear among the Top-100 market tokens). Consequently, taxonomy-technical demand shares are modest (e.g., 3.97\% of postings require at least one Programming skill), and the RQ3 deficits should be read as \textit{coverage} gaps conditional on demand rather than as economy-wide demand shares. The nested LinkedIn check (ALIGN04) shows both sides of this conditioning: deficits shrink further on free-text skills (Programming 1.39 pp), and exact keyword taxonomy matching under-detects demand in corpora whose skill strings are not pre-normalized---so comparisons across the two annotation layers are valid for the \textit{ordering} of deficits but not their absolute magnitudes.
    \item \textbf{Automated Severity Calibration:} Because absolute domain deficits are small, the Critical/Moderate/Mild labels use a relative-to-median rule rather than externally anchored thresholds; different anchors would reassign labels, though the ranking of categories is invariant.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Across 15 open datasets (1.35M real web-mined postings, 316K unverified aggregate rows, and 458K unverified-provenance benchmark rows), this empirical investigation---which proxies academic skill requirements with an employer-derived occupational reference profile rather than university syllabi---shows that profile--market misalignment is twofold. A substantial share of the apparent deficit reflects vocabulary divergence between academic descriptors and recruitment postings (the SGI falls from 52\% to 32\% under semantic alignment, and several domain deficits shrink by more than half), while substantive residual gaps remain concentrated in rapidly evolving technical areas, specifically Cloud Infrastructure and Programming \& Development. The instrument supporting this claim is examined three ways: expert-gold agreement on published ESCO mappings ($\kappa = 0.737$), a nested-superset robustness check on 1.29M postings from the same scrape family (both indices strictly positive; identical SGI at matched academic covering-set size), and a computational anchor to the CS2023 curricular guidelines (embedding coverage concentrated in applied knowledge areas; no human line-by-line topic audit was performed). In practical terms, addressing this divide does not necessitate wholesale curriculum redesign. Integrating modular credentials into established courses, requiring verifiable applied project portfolios, and adopting semantic matching in hiring workflows offer feasible steps toward aligning academic preparation with contemporary workforce demands.
